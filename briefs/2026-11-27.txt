EXPLAINER

What an end-user export control is, and why it reached Singapore
Most export controls are about destinations: you may not ship this chip to that country. An end-user control goes further and asks who actually uses the chip, wherever it sits. That matters for AI because computing power can be rented remotely. A Chinese company does not need Nvidia servers in Shanghai if it can log into a data centre in Johor or Singapore.
That was the gap. One think tank estimates remote data centres like these supply about a quarter of China's AI computing power. Earlier this month the US Commerce Department extended its rules to cover the users of advanced computing in cloud and colocation facilities, not just where hardware is shipped. Nvidia has now told Dell, Super Micro and others to pause server shipments to Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand and Indonesia while it checks whether its regional partners comply.
For Singapore the cost is real: data centres and cloud are part of its pitch as a neutral hub, and know-your-customer checks on compute buyers now become part of doing that business.
The takeaway for AI governance: control is moving from the box to the account.
