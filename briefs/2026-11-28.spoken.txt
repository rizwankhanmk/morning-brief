Today's explainer, What a trade secret is, and why Apple and OpenAI are fighting over people. A patent is public: you publish how your invention works and in return get about 20 years of exclusive rights. A trade secret is the opposite deal. Anything with value because competitors do not know it, such as a supplier list, a hardware roadmap or the tuning behind a product, is protected for as long as you keep it secret and take reasonable steps to guard it. There is no registration and no expiry, but once it leaks, the protection is largely gone. That is why trade secret fights tend to be about people and files rather than inventions. Apple sued OpenAI in July, saying former staff carried engineering details into OpenAI's device work. On Friday OpenAI countered that a worker who returned to Apple took its hardware plans with him, that Apple pressures suppliers working with OpenAI, and that Apple's practice of letting staff mix work and personal accounts lets it watch people after they leave. OpenAI says about 400 Apple employees have joined it; Apple's case goes to trial in July 2027. The hard part for a court is that experience itself cannot be owned. An engineer may take skills and general know-how to a rival, but not documents or specific secrets. Much of the evidence is therefore forensic: what was downloaded, when, and to which account. For anyone building AI products, or moving between AI companies, the lesson is practical: keep a clean line between what you know and what you were given, and expect hardware and device teams to be the most closely policed in the industry right now.
