One idea from today's news, in plain words

What an inference engine does, and why DeepSeek gave one away

Training a model is the expensive part everyone talks about. Serving it, which is called inference, is the part that costs money every single day. An inference engine is the software that takes a trained model and answers requests as cheaply and quickly as possible: it batches many users' prompts together, keeps the right numbers in the right memory, and skips work it can predict.

DeepSeek's DeepInfer is that software for its own models, released free under an open licence. Two things make that interesting. It runs on Huawei's Ascend chips as well as Nvidia's, which makes Chinese hardware a little more usable for everyone. And it pushes down the price of serving open models, which is the fight DeepSeek wants: if open models are cheap to run, closed rivals have to compete on something other than cost.

For a product person the takeaway is simple: model quality gets the headlines, but serving cost decides what you can afford to ship.

Policy and chips

Anthropic pitches an AI safety bill with a judge-approval step

Anthropic's draft federal bill, first reported by Politico, would let the government block a frontier model only for a foreseeable, imminent risk of more than US$1 billion in damage or 50 or more deaths, and only after the Commerce Department persuades a judge in Washington. No agency could act alone. Critics say the bar is far too high.
